I came to Acapulco for work. All day long I listened to conversations about telecommunications, messaging, and new channels of communication. The questions were the usual ones: how to reach further, how to deliver faster, how to capture the attention of users who are more and more saturated with information. When the conference ended I decided to walk along the beach for a while before heading back to the hotel. The sand still held some of the day's warmth. The wind carried the smell of the sea and, for a moment, the noise of the presentations, the screens, and the business talk began to fade away. All that remained were the waves advancing and retreating over the shore with an impossible patience.

There is something about the sea that forces you to think more slowly.

## Hemingway facing the sea

Perhaps that is why I ended up thinking of Hemingway. I have always admired his ability to find depth without resorting to complexity. While other writers seemed determined to prove how much they knew, he spent a good part of his life stripping away the unnecessary until he was left with something closer to the truth. He was a novelist, a correspondent, a tireless observer of the human condition. At heart, he was a man devoted to communication.

And as I walked along the bay I could not help imagining him facing another sea, decades ago, chasing the very same question that still chases us today: how to convey something important to another person.

## The harbor that was always a message

The conference I had come from was about the future of communication. Acapulco, however, seemed to tell a different story. Before telephones, before the internet, and long before data centers, this harbor already existed to connect distant worlds. Through these waters traveled goods, news, letters, stories, and dreams. Every ship that appeared on the horizon was, in a sense, a message.

Perhaps that is why the image of a bottle thrown into the sea remains so powerful. Because it represents something profoundly human. The hope that our words might cross the distance and find someone on the other side.

Come to think of it, our whole history could be described this way. An endless succession of messages. Some written on stone. Others carried by ships. Others printed on paper. Others turned into electrical impulses traveling through undersea cables. The technology changes, but the need remains.

We need to share ideas because no single person can discover the world alone. We live on inherited knowledge. We inhabit cities designed by people we will never meet. We use tools created by earlier generations. We understand the universe thanks to observations accumulated over centuries. All of that exists because someone found a way to carry an idea beyond their own life.
