I have always believed that a city reveals more about a civilization than any of its speeches. A walk of just a few kilometers is enough to understand what a society fears, what it admires, what it wishes to preserve, and what it is willing to sacrifice. There are cities built for contemplation and others for productivity; some were raised as monuments to power, while others seem resigned to mere survival. None of them is innocent. Architecture never is.

Perhaps that is why it fascinates me so much. Because even before speaking, human beings had already begun to build meaning.

The Gothic cathedrals were not only temples; they were stone attempting to touch the sky. Their infinite columns and impossible stained glass sought to diminish man before the divine. The Baroque, on the other hand, understood something different: power could also seduce. So came the excesses, the curves, the gold, the domes that seemed painted to convince the visitor he stood before something eternal. Much later, modernism tried to destroy all of that. Le Corbusier dreamed of rational cities where geometry would replace human chaos, while Mies van der Rohe reduced space until it became an almost religious phrase: "less is more".

But history never stays still. Then came brutalism, misunderstood and gigantic, raising masses of bare concrete as if the world had just survived a war and needed buildings capable of withstanding another. Many hated it. Others found in it a strange sincerity: structures where nothing tried to hide, where the pipes, the weight, and the material were displayed without shame. Something similar happened with Soviet architecture, which today is often observed with aesthetic disdain, forgetting that those constructions were born of brutal winters, impossible densities, and a collective obsession with functionality. They were not buildings designed for magazines; they were machines of human survival.

Meanwhile, in Japan, Tadao Ando built silences. Concrete walls where light seemed to enter like a prayer. Empty spaces that conveyed not absence, but calm. And in Barcelona, Antoni Gaudí refused to accept the straight line as the natural destiny of the world, allowing architecture to grow as if it were an organic extension of the earth itself. Decades later, Zaha Hadid would once again deform geometry, building structures that seemed to arrive from a future incapable of remaining still.

Each style responds to a distinct obsession. Permanence. Spirituality. Order. Collectivity. Serenity. Power. Efficiency. Beauty. None of them exists without reason.

## Technological modernity forgot that lesson

Today we build systems the way certain urban developers raise identical housing complexes on any territory, ignoring the climate, the wear, the density, or even the life that will unfold within them. Infrastructure is replicated the way one replicates concrete. Microservices for everything, distribution for everything, abstractions upon abstractions, as if complexity in itself were a sign of sophistication. As if every office had to look like a brutalist fortress or every product needed to become a metropolis impossible to maintain.

But no serious architect would choose the same material for a library, a museum, a theater, and an industrial complex. Louis Kahn said that a space should ask itself what it wanted to be before being built. And perhaps therein lies the difference between designing with intention and simply following a trend.

Because to build has always been an ethical act.

## The ethics of what we raise

A real architect must think about earthquakes, humidity, ventilation, fires, erosion, and time. He must imagine how his work will age when he no longer exists. He must ask himself whether people will be able to inhabit that space without feeling crushed by it. We too should do the same. After all, there are systems that end up resembling certain modern cities all too closely: enormous, impressive, profoundly complex… and utterly inhuman.

And even so, we keep building.

## The urge to leave something behind

Because perhaps that is one of the deepest urges of human beings: to leave something behind that outlives our own existence. We raise lighthouses to defy the sea, theaters to contain emotions, museums to protect memory, and skyscrapers to remind us that we have always wanted to touch something higher than ourselves. The Lighthouse of Alexandria did not only illuminate ships; it ended up giving its name to every lighthouse that would exist after it. The Sydney Opera House seemed impossible even before it was finished, and yet it became one of the most recognizable forms on the planet. The Eiffel Tower was criticized, ridiculed, and called monstrous before transforming into the symbol of an entire era.

That too is creating: daring to imagine something before the rest of the world understands why it should exist.

Sometimes we aspire to build the modern equivalent of the Louvre, a place capable of preserving ideas and works greater than ourselves. Other times we seek to raise immense structures where people enter and feel a strange inspiration before human scale pushed to its limit. And others, quite simply, we want to build a small cabin in the middle of the silence, a space where someone can breathe easy far from the noise of the world.

We work for clients, companies, and patrons, yes. But our judgment, our ethics, and our imagination should always push us toward something higher: to build safely, to build with intention, and to build to improve the lives of those who will inhabit our works.

Because in the end, all architecture —even the digital kind— ends up revealing what kind of civilization we wanted to be.
